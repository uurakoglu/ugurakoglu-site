Upgrades are mostly a people project: moving users to a new PLM release

Most of the effort in a PLM upgrade is not on the servers. A new release changes a screen, a menu or a saving habit, and for someone who has worked the same way for years, even a small change can feel like a loss of speed. That has been true in every upgrade and rollout I have worked on, including the 2024x upgrade and the DELMIA rollout. It is also useful to remember that people usually resist the interruption, not the improvement.

What helped

  • Explain why, in the user's terms. "The servers are outdated" means little to a design engineer. "Opening the vehicle assembly now takes nine minutes instead of about seventeen" does.
  • Start with volunteers. A small group from different departments tests early, finds the awkward details and then helps colleagues in their own units. Colleagues trust a peer more than a notice from IT.
  • Train by role, not by feature. People want to know how their own daily tasks change, so sessions are built around those tasks.
  • Stay close at go-live and after. Go-live sessions, quick answers and one-to-one help in the first weeks decide whether people accept the new release or work around it.
  • Measure and report back. Sharing the numbers after the switch turns an unwelcome change into something that people can see paid off.

Keeping a long project on track

The 2024x upgrade ran for 18 months. I followed it step by step in Jira and the project wiki, matched my own tasks to the calendar and supported the testing, documentation, training and go-live. A project that long is hard to hold together without a visible plan, and it was satisfying to see it finish: a production cutover over a single weekend, with no business downtime.

What I take from it

The technical plan and the human plan are one plan. When I scope an upgrade now, I give training, communication and post-go-live support the same attention as sizing, testing and rollback.
